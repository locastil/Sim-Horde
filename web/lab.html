How a variable gets in

  1. Quick check. Does the variable explain what the simulation gets wrong? We add it to the simulation's own happiness scores and see whether that predicts real life-satisfaction surveys better, for countries left out of the fit (averaged over 20 different splits). For migration, we check whether it helps predict real net migration.
  2. Full test. Variables that pass go inside the simulation itself. It runs the world on real 2015–2023 conditions, the strength is tuned on half the countries, then it's scored on the other half, which it never saw, and the halves swap.
  3. Live. Only a variable that beats the current model on countries it never saw goes into the shared world.

Most candidates fail, and that's the point: a test that every idea passes isn't a test. Scores below are correlations, where 1 is a perfect match and 0 is none; the quick-check numbers are how much a variable adds.

What we leave out, and why

  • Survey answers about the same things we score against. Questions like "do you have someone to count on?" or "are you free to make life choices?" come from the same Gallup poll as the happiness surveys. Adding them would raise the score without making the simulation any more realistic.
  • Religion and ethnicity. Too easy to misuse, and not something a simulation of ordinary lives should sort people by.
  • Housing costs. Only measured well in rich countries, so it would make the model better for them and blind everywhere else.

    Write it up for the idea desk, in as much detail as you like. Claude, an AI, will read it carefully and give you an honest evaluation: what's strong about it, where the data could come from, how it would change the simulation, what might get in the way, and how we'd test it. If it holds up, it goes to the developer with Claude's notes. You can add one follow-up, like a link or a detail. Two ideas per person a day.
